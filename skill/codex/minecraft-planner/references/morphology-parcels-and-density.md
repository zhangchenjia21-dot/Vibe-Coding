# morphology-parcels-and-density.md

## Purpose

This reference covers road, district, block, parcel, frontage and density morphology. It prevents modern zoning, empty-grid planning and “roads first, buildings later” layouts.

v0.4 strengthens three mechanisms:

- rights / access and Actor agency;
- qualitative site value / spatial competition;
- household / demographic processes and feedback.

---

## 1. Morphology is accumulated relation

At settlement scale, form emerges from:

```text
terrain / surface
+ anchor history
+ effective movement
+ ownership / rights
+ externality
+ institutional control
+ Actor competition / cooperation
+ household / inheritance / migration process
+ subdivision / infill
+ rebuilding / path dependence
```

Do not treat the final plan as a single simultaneous design event unless the world explicitly supports planned founding.

---

## 2. Roads and paths

Possible roles:

- primary inter-settlement road;
- market approach;
- ridge / riverbank route;
- freight path;
- service lane / rear alley;
- ritual / ceremonial route;
- military route;
- seasonal track.

A route should explain:

```text
origins
important destinations
primary flow
physical terrain reason
rights / permission when consequential
transport mode
historical stage
later changes
```

Avoid arbitrary branching just to look organic.

Organic does not mean random.

---

## 3. Effective access shapes morphology

A physical corridor may fail to become a public street if:

- access rights are absent;
- an institution controls the frontage;
- toll / jurisdiction redirects ordinary movement;
- animals / freight are excluded;
- seasonal conditions reduce reliability.

Conversely, an easement or shared passage can preserve a lane through private parcels.

Thus:

> **geometry explains where movement could happen; rights and users help explain where movement actually stabilizes.**

---

## 4. Commons and public space

A square / yard / open ground is not valid merely because empty space remains.

Possible causal origins:

- crossing / waiting;
- market overflow;
- loading / unloading;
- churchyard / ritual;
- assembly ground;
- common pasture;
- defensive clear area;
- water access / well court;
- institutional courtyard;
- bridgehead waiting area;
- seasonal / festival use.

Record:

```text
control / ownership
who may use it
who may exclude others
rhythm / peak use
why infill does not consume it
who could oppose enclosure
```

---

## 5. District formation without modern zoning

Historical clustering can occur because of:

- shared infrastructure;
- similar externalities;
- effective freight access;
- institutional jurisdiction;
- market frontage;
- water access;
- kinship / guild organization;
- defensive protection;
- inherited property boundaries;
- common rights;
- different relative site values.

Use relational language instead of color-block zoning:

- market-facing mixed frontage;
- workshop-heavy lane;
- warehouse edge;
- sacred precinct;
- residential infill behind primary frontage;
- shared production yards;
- institutional compound belt.

Mixed use remains plausible wherever separation is not causally required.

---

## 6. Block formation

Blocks can form from:

- paths enclosing space over time;
- walls / canals / terrain edges;
- subdivision of estates;
- planned colonization strips;
- market frontage growth;
- fortification constraints;
- irregular infill around earlier buildings;
- controlled institutional land resisting through-routes;
- new secondary lanes created by rear-access pressure.

Do not force rectangular blocks unless surveying tradition, planned foundation, military order, irrigation, terrain or land-allocation system supports them.

---

## 7. Parcel lifecycle as morphology, not engineering lifecycle

Possible parcel events:

- initial claim;
- boundary stabilization;
- household / inheritance split;
- sale / amalgamation;
- rear extension;
- frontage subdivision;
- courtyard sharing;
- service easement;
- institutional acquisition;
- fire rebuild;
- abandonment;
- migration / new household insertion;
- encroachment on commons;
- street widening taking frontage.

This is a **property / household history**, not an infrastructure maintenance schedule.

Important parcels in mature settlements should have plausible causal history.

---

## 8. Household and demographic mechanisms

Household count is not static.

Relevant spatial drivers may include:

- new household formation;
- inheritance division;
- household merger;
- incoming migrants;
- seasonal workers becoming permanent;
- apprentices / lodgers / dependents;
- relocation toward work / frontage.

Possible spatial consequences:

```text
household split
→ parcel division
→ shared courtyard / easement
→ rear infill / new lane
```

```text
seasonal lodging demand
→ repeated lodging use
→ permanent migrant households
→ new secondary cluster
```

Do not run a demographic microsimulation. Use the mechanism only when it explains form.

---

## 9. Relative site value

Different users value the same place differently.

Qualitative site value can derive from:

```text
effective accessibility
+ visibility
+ throughput
+ prestige / ritual significance
+ externality
+ rights / tenure security
+ available space
→ relative site value
```

Examples:

- merchant values market frontage highly;
- freight storage values loading access more than ceremonial visibility;
- quiet household may prefer slightly lower access to avoid animal / market disturbance;
- institution may retain a high-value frontage because ownership resists subdivision.

Do not invent money values unless the world actually supports them.

---

## 10. Spatial competition

When valuable frontage / ground is scarce, competition may create:

- lateral parcel subdivision;
- narrower frontage;
- deeper plots;
- greater coverage / verticality;
- rear extensions;
- shared alleys / courts;
- displacement of low-value uses;
- emergence of secondary frontage;
- institutional resistance / enclave effects.

Typical chain:

```text
primary route gains effective access + trade
→ frontage value rises
→ several households / merchants compete
→ wide plots subdivide laterally
→ narrow deep parcels
→ public-facing uses concentrate at front
→ residence / workshop / storage extend behind
→ rear access becomes valuable
```

Do not impose narrow plots merely because “old towns have narrow houses”.

---

## 11. Frontage

Ask:

- Which route matters and to whom?
- Is access legal / public / shared / restricted?
- What type of movement creates value?
- Is frontage continuous or broken?
- Does commerce or prestige compete for frontage?
- Do institutions monopolize segments?
- Are rear functions accessible?
- What later pressure changes this frontage?

---

## 12. Courtyards and shared service space

A courtyard may be household private, compound internal, shared, semi-public, loading, livestock, production, water-related, drainage / light well.

Explain:

- who can enter;
- who maintains control / access;
- what uses it;
- whether goods / animals move through it;
- what time rhythm matters;
- whether it is inherited void or deliberate shared resource.

---

## 13. Density is multidimensional

Useful variables:

- frontage continuity;
- parcel width / depth;
- coverage;
- verticality;
- shared walls;
- setback frequency;
- lane width / spacing;
- courtyard size;
- service-space compression;
- infill rate;
- secondary-building count;
- open-space hierarchy;
- edge permeability.

Density should be explained by pressure and mechanism:

```text
high effective access + scarce frontage + subdivision rights
→ narrower plots + greater continuity
```

or:

```text
institutional ownership + protected court
→ high central activity but low subdivision
```

Sparse and dense depend on scale.

---

## 14. Settlement edge

Possible edge types:

- gradual agricultural thinning;
- wall / ditch;
- floodplain stop;
- cliff / slope barrier;
- harbor edge;
- protected productive ground;
- forest edge;
- ribbon extension along road;
- satellite cluster;
- mine / workshop outlier;
- sacred / burial edge;
- access-right boundary.

Explain where expansion pressure goes and why it stops / redirects.

---

## 15. Feedback in morphology

Morphology can create new pressures.

Examples:

```text
frontage intensifies
→ congestion rises
→ side lane / bypass gains value
→ secondary frontage appears
```

```text
shared well court stabilizes
→ daily movement converges
→ surrounding parcels value access
→ court edges densify
```

```text
rear infill increases
→ rear access demand
→ service easement becomes lane
→ new parcel frontage emerges
```

Use feedback only when it changes the next stage.

---

## 16. Planned foundations are allowed when historically justified

Regular morphology may be appropriate for military colony, planned market town, camp-derived settlement, irrigation settlement, monastery, ruler-founded quarter, magical infrastructure etc.

But planning institution, surveying capability, authority and reason must exist.

---

## 17. Anti-Zoning Test

Hide labels such as residential / commercial / industrial.

Does geometry still express:

- effective flow;
- adjacency;
- externality;
- ownership / rights;
- historical growth;
- terrain / surface;
- competition?

If only colored zones make it legible, morphology is too zoning-driven.

---

## 18. Parcel Causality Test

For important parcel / parcel family, explain at least one or more:

- why this frontage width / depth;
- why rear access exists or not;
- why subdivision / amalgamation occurred;
- whether household / inheritance / migration caused change;
- whether site-value competition caused change;
- why a courtyard remained;
- why corner behavior differs;
- why an institution resisted subdivision;
- which rights / easements constrain geometry.

If no causal answer exists, parcel geometry may be arbitrary.

---

## 19. Terrain / Surface Necessity Test

Move the same plan onto different but similarly sized terrain / ground character.

If almost nothing changes despite claimed adaptation, revisit movement, effective access, drainage, slope, ground character, retaining / crossing logic and productive-ground preservation.
