# morphology-parcels-and-density.md

## Purpose

This reference covers road, district, block, parcel, frontage and density morphology. It exists to prevent modern zoning, empty-grid planning and “roads first, buildings later” layouts.

---

## 1. Morphology is accumulated relation

At settlement scale, form emerges from:

```text
terrain
+ anchor history
+ movement
+ ownership
+ externality
+ institutional control
+ subdivision / infill
+ rebuilding
```

Do not treat the final plan as a single simultaneous design event unless the world explicitly supports planned founding.

---

## 2. Roads and paths

Possible roles:

- primary inter-settlement road;
- market approach;
- ridge route;
- riverbank route;
- freight path;
- service lane;
- rear alley;
- ritual / ceremonial route;
- military route;
- seasonal track.

A route should have:

```text
origins
important destinations
primary flow
terrain reason
historical stage
later changes
```

Avoid arbitrary branching just to look organic.

Organic does not mean random.

---

## 3. Commons and public space

A square / yard / open ground is not valid merely because empty space remains.

Possible causal origins:

- widening at crossing;
- market overflow;
- loading / unloading;
- churchyard;
- assembly ground;
- common pasture;
- defensive clear area;
- water access;
- courtyard of an institution;
- bridgehead waiting area;
- festival / ritual use.

Record ownership / control and why infill does not consume it.

---

## 4. District formation without modern zoning

Historical clustering can occur because of:

- shared infrastructure;
- similar externalities;
- access to freight;
- institutional jurisdiction;
- market frontage;
- water access;
- kinship / guild organization;
- defensive protection;
- inherited property boundaries.

Use relational language instead of color-block zoning when possible:

- market-facing mixed frontage;
- workshop-heavy lane;
- warehouse edge;
- sacred precinct;
- residential infill behind primary frontage;
- shared production yards;
- institutional compound belt.

Mixed use should remain plausible wherever separation is not causally required.

---

## 5. Block formation

Blocks can form from:

- paths enclosing space over time;
- walls / canals / terrain edges;
- subdivision of estates;
- planned colonization strips;
- market frontage growth;
- fortification constraints;
- irregular infill around earlier buildings.

Do not force rectangular blocks unless terrain, surveying tradition, planned foundation, military order or land-allocation system supports them.

---

## 6. Parcel lifecycle

Possible parcel events:

- initial claim;
- boundary stabilization;
- inheritance split;
- sale / amalgamation;
- rear extension;
- frontage subdivision;
- courtyard sharing;
- service easement;
- institutional acquisition;
- fire rebuild;
- abandonment;
- encroachment on commons;
- street widening taking part of frontage.

Important parcels should have a plausible lifecycle, especially in mature dense settlements.

---

## 7. Frontage

Frontage has value because it interfaces with movement and visibility.

Ask:

- Which route matters?
- What kind of access is valuable?
- Is frontage continuous or broken?
- Does commerce compete for frontage?
- Do large institutions monopolize a frontage segment?
- Are rear functions still accessible?

Typical causal chain:

```text
primary route becomes commercially valuable
→ frontage demand rises
→ wide plots subdivide laterally
→ narrow deep parcels
→ public-facing activity moves to front
→ residence / workshop / storage extend behind
→ rear access becomes valuable
```

Do not impose narrow plots purely because “old towns have narrow houses”.

---

## 8. Courtyards and shared service space

A courtyard may be:

- household private;
- compound internal;
- shared by several parcels;
- semi-public service court;
- loading yard;
- livestock yard;
- production yard;
- drainage / light well.

Explain:

- who can enter;
- what uses it;
- whether goods / animals move through it;
- whether it is an inherited void or intentional shared resource.

---

## 9. Density is multidimensional

Do not represent density only as “more buildings”.

Useful morphology variables:

- frontage continuity;
- parcel width;
- parcel depth;
- building coverage;
- number of floors / verticality;
- shared walls;
- setback frequency;
- lane width;
- lane spacing;
- courtyard size;
- service-space compression;
- infill rate;
- secondary building count;
- open-space hierarchy;
- edge permeability.

Examples:

### Dense mixed gateway

May express density through:

- continuous frontage;
- narrow plots;
- rear service access;
- compact shared yards;
- upper floors;
- mixed commercial-residential use;
- short warehouses embedded behind street frontage.

### Sparse mountain settlement

May still have locally dense clusters around scarce terraces while remaining regionally dispersed.

Thus “sparse” and “dense” depend on scale.

---

## 10. Settlement edge

Edges are not always circles around a town.

Possible edge types:

- gradual agricultural thinning;
- wall / ditch;
- floodplain stop;
- cliff / slope barrier;
- harbor edge;
- forest production edge;
- ribbon extension along road;
- detached satellite cluster;
- mine / workshop outlier;
- sacred / burial edge.

Explain where expansion pressure goes and where it is blocked.

---

## 11. Planned foundations are allowed when historically justified

The Skill is anti-unconscious-modern-planning, not anti-planning.

Regular morphology may be appropriate for:

- military colony;
- planned market town;
- camp-derived settlement;
- irrigation settlement;
- institutional monastery compound;
- ruler-founded capital quarter;
- magical infrastructure requiring geometric order.

But the planning institution, surveying capability and reason must exist.

---

## 12. Anti-Zoning Test

Hide labels such as “residential / commercial / industrial”.

Ask whether the geometry still expresses:

- flow;
- adjacency;
- externality;
- ownership;
- historical growth;
- terrain.

If the only legibility comes from four colored zones, the morphology is too zoning-driven.

---

## 13. Terrain Necessity Test

Translate or rotate the same plan onto a different but similarly sized terrain.

If almost nothing needs to change despite claiming strong terrain adaptation, revisit:

- movement resistance;
- buildable pockets;
- drainage;
- slope;
- visibility;
- crossing points;
- terrace / ridge logic.

---

## 14. Parcel Causality Test

For each important parcel or parcel family, explain at least one of:

- why this frontage width;
- why this depth;
- why rear access exists or does not;
- why subdivision happened;
- why amalgamation happened;
- why a courtyard remained;
- why a corner behaves differently;
- why an institution resisted subdivision.

If no causal answer exists, parcel geometry may be arbitrary.
